# Redefining Morality: A Secular Vocabulary for Ethics

*Triad of Stewardship | Essays*

Growing up in Chad and the Central African Republic, I was immersed in the strict teachings of Fundamentalist Baptist Christianity. English was my second language, but the real linguistic challenge came later, when I began questioning the religious framework that shaped my moral vocabulary. After transitioning to Evangelicalism in my twenties and eventually deconstructing my faith entirely, I now identify as an agnostic with strong atheistic leanings. Yet my commitment to living an ethical life remains unwavering. Words like "righteousness" or "sin," however, feel like relics of a worldview I no longer hold — they carry the weight of sermons, divine judgment, and a past I've left behind.

If you've ever struggled to express your moral values without falling back on religious terms, you're not alone. Many of us who've moved away from religious roots find ourselves searching for a new language to articulate ethics. The words we inherit shape how we think, and untangling them from religious connotations can feel like unlearning a mother tongue. This essay offers practical, secular alternatives to describe ethical concepts, grounded in human well-being and observable actions.

## Describing Positive Actions and Character

Religious terms often imply a spiritual state judged by a higher power, but secular ethics calls for language that reflects tangible, human-centered qualities.

**Integrity** speaks to moral wholeness and consistency — someone who aligns their actions with their values, even when no one is watching.

**Virtue**, rooted in philosophy rather than religion, refers to morally good traits like honesty, compassion, or courage. If "virtue" feels too formal, specify the trait: "she acted with remarkable compassion."

**Moral courage** highlights the strength to act ethically in the face of opposition — the whistleblower who risks their job to expose unethical practices.

**Ethical living** is a broad, neutral term for a life guided by principles — choosing to buy fair-trade products to support sustainable practices.

**Conscientious** describes someone thoughtful who feels a moral obligation to do right.

**Principled** applies to someone who adheres to clear ethical guidelines and holds to them under pressure.

These terms shift the focus from an abstract, spiritual ideal to concrete practices that anyone can observe and adopt.

## Shifting to Secular Morality

The heart of this linguistic shift is moving from a framework of divine command to one of human flourishing. By focusing on well-being, we can build a robust, compassionate moral framework. Three questions guide the reorientation:

- Does this action promote well-being for all involved?
- What are the consequences of this behavior on the community?
- Is this a sustainable way to live with others?

These questions anchor ethics in practical, earthly outcomes. Focusing on human flourishing forces us to consider real people, not abstract ideals.

## A New Ethical Language

Redefining your moral vocabulary is a journey, not a destination. It takes time to unlearn the religious terms that once shaped your worldview and replace them with words that reflect your current values.

The next time you're tempted to call something "sinful," pause and consider: what harm is being done? Or when you want to praise someone's "righteousness," ask: what specific quality — integrity, courage, compassion — am I actually admiring? These small shifts can transform how you think and talk about ethics.

We can craft a language of ethics that is inclusive, grounded, and deeply human. We don't need the promise of heaven to be good. We only need to recognize that being good builds a world we all want to live in.
