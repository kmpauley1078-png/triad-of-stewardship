# The Universal Core of Ethics
## What Humanity Keeps Agreeing On

Most ethical frameworks answer the question: *what should we value?* The Universal Core of Ethics asks a different question: *what do human ethical traditions actually agree on, when you strip away the theology, the cultural packaging, and the metaphysical claims, and look only at what they require people to do?*

The answer is more specific — and more useful — than most people expect.

---

## How It Was Built

Twenty-three ethical traditions were surveyed: from ancient Egyptian moral philosophy (c. 2400 BCE) through Zoroastrianism, Hinduism, Judaism, Daoism, Jainism, Buddhism, and Confucianism; through Aristotelian virtue ethics, Stoicism, Christianity, and Islam; through Norse ethics, Sikhism, and Baha'i; through Kantian deontology, Utilitarianism, and Secular Humanism; and including indigenous traditions from sub-Saharan Africa (Ubuntu, Bakongo), the pre-Columbian Americas (Aztec/Nahua), and pre-contact North America (Anishinaabe). Together these traditions account for the ethical frameworks of more than ninety-eight percent of all human beings who have ever lived.

The methodology was designed to prevent any single tradition from dominating the result. Each tradition was mapped independently — its conduct requirements stripped of theological and cosmological framing and stated in behavioral terms — before any cross-tradition comparison was made. The clusters emerged from that comparison. They were not imposed on it.

Six clusters of behavioral obligation appear across every tradition examined, independently of theology, geography, culture, and era. These are the six mandates.

---

## The Six Mandates

The mandates are not a ranked list. They are an interdependent system — and the interdependence has teeth:

*Mandate I without Mandate II produces protected slaves. Mandate II without Mandate III produces autonomous predators. Mandate III without Mandate IV produces honest agents inside collapsing systems. Mandate IV without Mandate V produces well-maintained institutions staffed by people who stop functioning when conditions get hard. Mandate V without Mandate VI produces sustainable complicity with tyranny.*

The framework requires all six.

**Mandate I — Universal Protection**
The obligation to prevent the termination or irreversible degradation of sentient agents. This is the floor beneath which no ethical justification can reach. The Zero-Point Rule — the protection of life and agency as the irreducible basis of all obligation — anchors everything else.

**Mandate II — Agency and Autonomy**
The requirement to respect and actively enable the self-determination of sentient beings: their capacity for genuine consent, independent reasoning, political participation, and freedom from coercion. Survival without self-direction is not enough.

**Mandate III — Integrity and Reciprocity**
The commitment to honesty, fairness, and mutual accountability — in communication, in exchange, in the keeping of commitments, and in the resolution of conflict. Technically true statements designed to produce false conclusions are violations. Fairness in appearance but not in outcome is a violation.

**Mandate IV — Systemic Stewardship**
The duty to maintain the health of the systems on which agents depend — economic, legal, informational, institutional, and environmental — including for people not yet present to defend their own interests. Individual integrity is not sufficient if the systems people live inside are being hollowed out.

**Mandate V — Sustained Ethical Function**
The obligation to act rightly independent of emotional state, relational pressure, or personal cost. These are the operational virtues — intellectual rigor, courage, temperance, humility — that make it possible to keep discharging the other mandates when circumstances make it hardest to do so.

**Mandate VI — Just Subversion**
The obligation to dissent from, expose, and where necessary dismantle systems that have turned predatory. This is the framework's self-defending mechanism. Without it, the other five mandates can be weaponized by any sufficiently cynical authority demanding compliance in the name of stability or order. Every tradition examined contains a structurally identical claim: legitimate authority has limits, and compliance with tyranny in the name of order is itself a moral failure.

## The Forty-Two Principles

Each mandate contains seven principles — specific, actionable conduct requirements precise enough to govern real decisions under real pressure. The principles are not aspirational. They generate distinct behavioral requirements in nearly every combination.

When all 861 pairwise interactions of the forty-two principles are mapped, the framework resolves 98.3% of cases with cross-traditional consensus. The remaining 1.7% are genuine unresolved voids — places where the traditions themselves disagree — and the framework marks them honestly rather than fabricating resolution where none exists.

The full principle set, with boundary conditions and application notes, is in the foundational documentation.

## The Priority Hierarchy

When mandates conflict — when protecting one person's safety requires constraining another person's autonomy, for example — the Priority Hierarchy provides the resolution logic. It does not rank the mandates against each other as a standing order. It provides specific, documented rules for the cases where genuine conflict arises.

Four named conflict-resolution rules govern the most common tension types:

- **Physics over Performance** — physical constraints override performance claims
- **Justice Before Harmony** — corrective justice takes precedence over social stability
- **Immediate Harm Over Abstract Virtue** — concrete present harm overrides theoretical principle
- **Truth Before Humility** — accurate information takes precedence over social comfort

## Why This Matters for AI Alignment

The alignment field needs external anchors — ethical standards that do not depend on any particular training distribution, cultural context, or political authority. The UCE is the most empirically grounded cross-cultural ethical standard currently documented. It does not derive from any single tradition's authority. It derives from the overlap of what humanity has independently and repeatedly concluded matters, across five thousand years, on every inhabited continent.

It also knows its own edges. The void zones — all clustering around a single unresolved root conflict between individual cognitive and political sovereignty and community authority claims — are not failures. They are the framework's accurate map of where confident guidance ends. A system that marks its own uncertainty is safer than one that doesn't.
